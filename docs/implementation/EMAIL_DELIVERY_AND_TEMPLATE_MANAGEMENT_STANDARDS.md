## 1. Purpose

This document defines standards for sending, tracking, securing, and managing email communications generated by the multi-store retail platform.

It establishes consistent requirements for transactional email, account notifications, store communications, operational alerts, templates, provider integrations, delivery failures, and email-related audit records.

## 2. Scope

These standards apply to:

- Customer transactional emails.
- Order confirmations.
- Payment and refund notifications.
- Shipping and fulfilment notifications.
- Account verification and recovery emails.
- Store administration notifications.
- Platform administration notifications.
- Employee invitations.
- Operational and security alerts.
- Approved marketing email integrations.
- Email templates and reusable content.
- Email provider configuration and monitoring.

Email must not become an independent source of truth for orders, payments, account status, or other platform records.

## 3. Core Principles

### 3.1 Reliable Communication

Email delivery must be traceable and recoverable where practical.

The platform must distinguish between an email being requested, queued, accepted by a provider, delivered, bounced, or rejected.

### 3.2 Store Isolation

Store-specific email must use the correct store context, branding, contact details, and approved configuration.

One store must not receive another store's private notifications or customer information.

### 3.3 Data Minimisation

Email content must contain only information required for its purpose.

Sensitive credentials, payment secrets, and unnecessary personal information must never be included.

### 3.4 Template Governance

Templates must be centrally governed, version-controlled, and tested.

Store-specific presentation may use approved configuration, but must not bypass platform security or communication rules.

### 3.5 Delivery Is Not Business Completion

A successful email provider response does not prove that the recipient has read or acted on the message.

Business processes must remain independent of email delivery.

## 4. Email Categories

The platform must distinguish email categories because they have different consent, security, and operational requirements.

### 4.1 Transactional Email

Transactional email supports an existing business operation.

Examples include:

- Order confirmation.
- Payment receipt.
- Refund confirmation.
- Shipping update.
- Account verification.
- Password recovery.
- Security notification.
- Store employee invitation.

Transactional email must be generated from authoritative platform events or approved workflows.

### 4.2 Operational Email

Operational email supports platform or store administration.

Examples include:

- Integration failure alerts.
- Scheduled job failure notifications.
- Reconciliation exceptions.
- Security alerts.
- Store onboarding notifications.
- Administrative approval requests.

Operational email must be sent only to authorised recipients.

### 4.3 Marketing Email

Marketing email promotes products, offers, or commercial activity.

It requires a separately governed consent, preference, unsubscribe, and legal compliance process.

Transactional consent must not be interpreted as marketing consent.

Marketing campaigns must not be introduced through ordinary transactional email templates.

## 5. Email Provider Architecture

The platform must use an approved email delivery provider.

The integration architecture must define:

- Provider identity.
- API authentication.
- Sending domains.
- Sender identities.
- Delivery endpoints.
- Webhook handling.
- Retry behaviour.
- Rate limits.
- Provider failure handling.
- Monitoring.
- Credential rotation.

Provider-specific logic should be isolated behind an application service or adapter.

Business services should request email delivery through the platform's notification capabilities rather than calling provider APIs directly.

### 5.1 Current and Target Architecture

The current platform uses Netlify Functions and Supabase.

Resend may be used as the approved email delivery provider once its domain, API credentials, sender identities, and production configuration have been verified.

The planned NestJS modular monolith should preserve the provider abstraction and notification contracts.

A provider change must not require rewriting unrelated commerce or administration workflows.

### 5.2 Provider Configuration

Provider configuration must be environment-specific.

Development, staging, and production must not accidentally share sending credentials or production sender identities.

Production sending must use verified domains and approved sender addresses.

## 6. Sender Identity and Domain Configuration

The platform must maintain approved sender identities.

Configuration must define:

- From name.
- From email address.
- Reply-to address.
- Sending domain.
- Environment.
- Store or platform ownership.
- Permitted email categories.

Store-specific sender identities may be supported where the domain and provider configuration have been approved.

A store must not use another store's sender identity without explicit authorisation.

### 6.1 Domain Authentication

Production sending domains must be configured with the provider's required authentication records.

The platform should configure and monitor:

- SPF.
- DKIM.
- DMARC.

Configuration must follow the provider's current technical requirements.

DMARC policy changes must be managed carefully to avoid disrupting legitimate mail from other authorised systems.

### 6.2 Sender Verification

Do not send production email from unverified domains or arbitrary user-supplied sender addresses.

Recipient-controlled input must never be used directly as the sender identity.

## 7. Template Management

Email templates must be maintained as version-controlled application assets or through an approved template-management system.

Each template must define:

- Unique template identifier.
- Purpose.
- Email category.
- Supported locale.
- Subject.
- HTML content.
- Plain-text content.
- Required variables.
- Optional variables.
- Store context requirements.
- Version.
- Owner.
- Approval status.

Templates must not contain hard-coded secrets or private provider credentials.

### 7.1 Reusable Layouts

Shared layouts should define consistent:

- Header structure.
- Footer structure.
- Typography.
- Spacing.
- Brand presentation.
- Accessibility features.
- Responsive behaviour.

Store-specific branding must use approved configuration values.

### 7.2 Template Variables

Template variables must be explicitly declared and validated.

Variables must be escaped according to their output context.

Untrusted customer, product, or store content must not be inserted as executable HTML.

Missing required variables must cause a controlled failure rather than silently generating misleading content.

### 7.3 Template Versioning

Changes to templates must be reviewed and version-controlled.

Where email content has financial, legal, security, or account implications, retain enough information to establish which template version was used.

Previously sent email records must not be silently rewritten when a template changes.

## 8. Store-Specific Email Configuration

Each participating store may have approved configuration for:

- Store display name.
- Logo.
- Brand colours.
- Contact details.
- Reply-to address.
- Storefront URL.
- Approved footer content.
- Supported communication preferences.

The platform must supply these values from verified store configuration.

A client must not be allowed to override trusted sender details or insert arbitrary links into system-generated email.

### 8.1 Store Context

Every store-specific email request must resolve the store through trusted server-side context.

The system must not trust a store identifier supplied only in the request body.

If store attribution cannot be established, the email must not be sent using guessed branding or recipient details.

### 8.2 Platform Communications

Platform-wide operational and security emails must use platform-owned sender identities and templates.

Store branding must not override platform security communications.

## 9. Recipient Validation

Email recipients must be resolved from authorised platform records or explicitly validated user input.

The application must validate:

- Recipient address format.
- Recipient ownership where required.
- Recipient permissions for operational messages.
- Store membership where relevant.
- Communication preferences.
- Applicable consent requirements.

A user must not be able to request arbitrary emails containing another user's private account or transaction information.

Recipient lists must be bounded and validated.

## 10. Sensitive Information

Email is not an appropriate channel for transmitting unrestricted secrets.

Never include:

- Passwords.
- Access tokens.
- Refresh tokens.
- API secrets.
- Database credentials.
- Payment provider credentials.
- Full payment card details.
- Private signing keys.
- Recovery secrets in ordinary notification messages.

Account recovery and verification should use short-lived, purpose-specific links or codes.

Such links and codes must have defined expiry, single-use behaviour where practical, and secure server-side validation.

### 10.1 Personal and Financial Information

Email content should minimise personal and financial information.

Where a message refers to a transaction, include only the details needed to identify and understand it.

Do not expose unnecessary customer addresses, internal notes, supplier information, or financial account details.

## 11. Delivery Workflow

Email delivery should follow a controlled workflow:

1. An authorised business operation requests an email.
2. The application validates the request and recipient.
3. The platform resolves store and template context.
4. Required template variables are validated.
5. An email delivery record is created.
6. The message is queued where asynchronous processing is used.
7. A worker submits the message to the provider.
8. The provider response is recorded.
9. Delivery events are processed where available.
10. Failures are retried or escalated according to policy.

The system must distinguish message creation from provider acceptance and final delivery status.

### 11.1 Durable Queueing

Where email delivery is asynchronous, the message request must be durably stored before the initiating business workflow assumes responsibility for delivery.

An application process restart must not silently discard queued messages.

### 11.2 Business Transaction Independence

An email failure must not reverse a successfully committed order or payment.

The platform must preserve the business transaction and retry or escalate the communication separately.

## 12. Idempotency and Duplicate Prevention

The platform must prevent duplicate email delivery where repeated business events or job execution could generate duplicate messages.

Use a stable notification identity based on the business event and intended communication.

The deduplication strategy must distinguish:

- Repeated processing of the same event.
- Legitimate separate notifications for different events.
- Deliberate resending by an authorised administrator.

A manual resend must be recorded separately and must not erase the original delivery history.

Provider idempotency features should be used where supported.

## 13. Retry and Failure Handling

The platform must define retry rules for transient provider failures.

The policy must specify:

- Retryable failures.
- Non-retryable failures.
- Maximum attempts.
- Backoff strategy.
- Maximum retry duration.
- Final failure state.
- Escalation procedure.

Examples of transient failures include temporary network interruptions and provider service unavailability.

Examples of non-retryable failures include invalid recipient addresses and permanently rejected sender identities.

A failed message must remain visible to authorised operators.

Retry exhaustion must not cause unlimited repeated sending.

## 14. Delivery Status and Provider Webhooks

Where the provider supports delivery webhooks, the platform may record:

- Accepted.
- Sent.
- Delivered.
- Bounced.
- Rejected.
- Deferred.
- Complained.
- Suppressed.

Provider webhook events must follow the platform's webhook authentication, deduplication, and durable receipt standards.

Delivery status must be linked to the correct internal email record using verified provider references.

A provider webhook must not be allowed to modify unrelated user, order, or financial records.

### 14.1 Status Semantics

The platform must document the meaning of each status.

Provider acceptance must not be reported as confirmed delivery.

Confirmed delivery must not be interpreted as proof that the recipient opened or read the email.

Open and click tracking must be used only where justified and permitted by the applicable privacy and communication rules.

## 15. Bounce and Complaint Management

Repeated hard bounces and spam complaints must be handled through controlled suppression rules.

The platform must distinguish:

- Permanent address failures.
- Temporary delivery failures.
- Provider rejections.
- Recipient complaints.
- User-initiated unsubscribes.

Marketing suppression and transactional delivery policies must remain distinct.

A marketing unsubscribe must not automatically prevent legally or operationally necessary transactional communications unless the applicable rules require it.

Suppression decisions must be auditable and reversible only through an authorised process.

## 16. Email Security

Email delivery must enforce:

- Secure provider authentication.
- TLS for provider API communication.
- Secret storage outside frontend code.
- Restricted provider permissions.
- Recipient validation.
- Template output escaping.
- Safe link generation.
- Controlled attachment handling.
- Rate limits.
- Abuse prevention.
- Audit logging.

Email templates must not accept arbitrary executable content from users.

### 16.1 Links

Links in system-generated emails must use approved HTTPS origins.

Sensitive actions must use short-lived, purpose-specific tokens.

Redirect destinations must be validated to prevent open redirect vulnerabilities.

### 16.2 Attachments

Attachments must be used only where necessary.

The platform must validate file type and size, control access to source files, and avoid attaching sensitive records unnecessarily.

Where a secure authenticated download is more appropriate, use a protected link with suitable expiry and access checks.

## 17. Accessibility and Rendering

Email templates must be usable across common email clients and screen sizes.

Templates should include:

- Semantic structure.
- Readable text.
- Sufficient colour contrast.
- Descriptive link text.
- Alternative text for meaningful images.
- A plain-text alternative.
- Mobile-friendly layout.
- Clear identification of the sender.

Important information must not be communicated through colour or images alone.

Avoid relying on unsupported interactive email features for essential workflows.

## 18. Marketing Communication Governance

Marketing communications require a separate consent and preference model.

The platform must define:

- How consent is collected.
- What communication the consent covers.
- How consent is recorded.
- How preferences are updated.
- How unsubscribe requests are processed.
- How suppression lists are maintained.
- How consent evidence is retained.

Store-specific marketing consent must not be automatically transferred to another participating store.

The platform must obtain appropriate South African legal and privacy review before launching marketing email functionality.

## 19. Logging and Audit

Email records must support operational investigation without exposing message secrets.

Record appropriate metadata, including:

- Internal message identifier.
- Notification type.
- Template identifier and version.
- Store context.
- Recipient reference.
- Provider reference.
- Creation timestamp.
- Submission timestamp.
- Delivery status.
- Retry count.
- Failure category.
- Initiating user or service identity.

Avoid logging full message bodies and unnecessary recipient information.

Sensitive message content must be protected through appropriate access controls and retention rules.

Administrative resends, suppression changes, and sender configuration changes must be auditable.

## 20. Monitoring and Reporting

Email operations must monitor:

- Messages queued.
- Messages submitted.
- Provider acceptance failures.
- Delivery failures.
- Bounce rates.
- Complaint rates.
- Retry volume.
- Queue age.
- Provider latency.
- Suppression activity.
- Template rendering failures.

Alerts should be based on operational impact and approved thresholds.

Reports must distinguish platform-wide delivery health from store-specific delivery performance.

Store administrators must not gain access to another store's recipient or message data through reporting.

## 21. Environment and Testing

Development and staging must use approved test recipients or provider test modes where available.

Production customer addresses must not be used for routine development testing.

Before release, test:

- Template rendering.
- Plain-text output.
- Required variable validation.
- Store branding.
- Recipient resolution.
- Provider authentication.
- Queue persistence.
- Retry behaviour.
- Duplicate prevention.
- Bounce processing.
- Webhook verification.
- Link validity.
- Mobile rendering.
- Accessibility.
- Sensitive data leakage.

Production sending must require explicit environment configuration and verified sender identities.

## 22. Retention and Data Protection

Email delivery records and message content must follow the platform's data retention and privacy standards.

Retention requirements must distinguish:

- Delivery metadata.
- Provider event records.
- Message content.
- Consent evidence.
- Suppression records.
- Financial or legal communication records.

The platform must retain only what is justified by operational, legal, financial, or compliance requirements.

Expired message content must be securely disposed of according to the approved retention procedure.

## 23. Operational Recovery

The platform must document procedures for:

- Provider outages.
- Domain authentication failures.
- Credential compromise.
- Queue backlog.
- Delivery rejection spikes.
- Template defects.
- Incorrect store attribution.
- Accidental duplicate sending.
- Recipient data exposure.

Provider credentials must be revocable and rotatable.

If a template or sender configuration is defective, operators must be able to disable the affected communication safely without disrupting unrelated platform operations.

## 24. Acceptance Criteria

An email implementation is acceptable when:

1. An approved provider is configured for each environment.
2. Production sender domains are verified.
3. Sender identities are controlled.
4. Email categories are separated.
5. Templates are version-controlled.
6. Required variables are validated.
7. Store context is resolved server-side.
8. Recipient access is authorised.
9. Sensitive credentials are excluded from messages.
10. Delivery requests are durably recorded.
11. Duplicate delivery is controlled.
12. Retry and failure handling are implemented.
13. Provider delivery events are authenticated.
14. Bounce and complaint handling is defined.
15. Marketing consent is managed separately.
16. Email operations are monitored and auditable.
17. Retention and access controls are applied.
18. Security, rendering, and delivery tests pass.

## 25. Open Decisions

The following decisions require explicit approval:

- Final email provider and account structure.
- Verified platform sending domains.
- Whether stores may use independent sending domains.
- Approved sender identities.
- Transactional email catalogue.
- Marketing email scope and consent model.
- Template ownership and approval workflow.
- Delivery retry limits and backoff values.
- Delivery metadata and content retention periods.
- Provider webhook event requirements.
- Attachment policy.
- Delivery monitoring thresholds.
- Whether store administrators may request manual resends.

Until approved, implementations must use verified sender identities, existing platform notification rules, and the approved secrets management process.
