## 1. Purpose

This policy establishes a consistent framework for identifying, assessing, recording, treating and monitoring risks across the platform and its business operations.

The objective is to protect the parent company, Store Owners, customers, suppliers, employees and the long-term sustainability of the platform.

Risk management must be part of normal business planning and decision-making, not an activity performed only after something goes wrong.

## 2. Scope

This policy applies to:

- The parent company.
- Platform management.
- Store Owners.
- Employees and contractors.
- Technology and infrastructure teams.
- Finance and reconciliation teams.
- Marketing and creative operations.
- Suppliers and third-party service providers.

It covers strategic, financial, operational, legal, regulatory, technology, security, privacy, commercial and reputational risks.

## 3. Risk management principles

Risk management must follow these principles:

1. Proactive identification: risks should be identified before they become incidents where reasonably possible.
2. Accountability: every material risk must have an assigned owner.
3. Evidence-based assessment: risk ratings must be supported by documented reasoning.
4. Proportionality: controls should reflect the likelihood and potential impact of a risk.
5. Continuous monitoring: risks must be reassessed when circumstances change.
6. Transparency: material risks must be communicated to the people responsible for managing them.
7. Practical treatment: risk responses must be achievable and measurable.
8. Business continuity: critical operations must have appropriate contingency arrangements.

## 4. Risk categories

The parent company should maintain a central risk register covering the following categories.

### 4.1 Strategic risks

Examples include:

- Weak market demand.
- Unsustainable commercial pricing.
- Poor Store Owner recruitment.
- High Store Owner turnover.
- Dependence on a small number of major stores.
- Failure to achieve the platform's business objectives.
- Competitive pressure.
- Expansion beyond available management capacity.

### 4.2 Financial risks

Examples include:

- Insufficient operating cash.
- Unpaid customer orders.
- Payment fraud.
- Settlement errors.
- Uncontrolled operating costs.
- Unexpected infrastructure expenditure.
- Chargebacks and refunds.
- Unrecoverable debts.
- Revenue concentration.
- Incorrect financial reporting.

### 4.3 Operational risks

Examples include:

- Fulfilment failures.
- Inventory inaccuracies.
- Supplier disruption.
- Poor customer support.
- Inadequate staff capacity.
- Incorrect product information.
- Failed marketing campaigns.
- Inconsistent Store Owner onboarding.
- Unclear operational responsibilities.

### 4.4 Technology risks

Examples include:

- Platform outages.
- Data loss.
- Failed deployments.
- Integration failures.
- Database corruption.
- Infrastructure capacity limitations.
- Dependency failures.
- Software vulnerabilities.
- Inadequate monitoring.
- Unsupported or outdated technology.

### 4.5 Cybersecurity and privacy risks

Examples include:

- Account compromise.
- Credential theft.
- Unauthorised access.
- Personal information exposure.
- Malicious software.
- Payment fraud.
- Insider misuse.
- Inadequate access controls.
- Third-party security incidents.
- Failure to meet POPIA obligations.

### 4.6 Legal and regulatory risks

Examples include:

- Consumer protection non-compliance.
- Privacy and data protection failures.
- Incorrect business representations.
- Intellectual property disputes.
- Employment-related disputes.
- Tax and financial reporting failures.
- Sector-specific product restrictions.
- Inadequate contractual documentation.

### 4.7 Reputational risks

Examples include:

- Poor customer experiences.
- Misleading advertising.
- Unresolved complaints.
- Unethical conduct.
- Public data breaches.
- Supplier misconduct.
- Store Owner disputes.
- Inconsistent service quality.

### 4.8 Commercial and partner risks

Examples include:

- Supplier failure.
- Third-party service interruption.
- Unclear service agreements.
- Uncontrolled marketing expenditure.
- Disputes over creative ownership.
- Misunderstood Store Owner responsibilities.
- Commercial dependency on a single service provider.

## 5. Risk identification

Risks should be identified through:

- Business planning.
- Platform development and releases.
- Security reviews.
- Financial reconciliation.
- Customer complaints.
- Store Owner feedback.
- Supplier assessments.
- Operational reviews.
- Internal audits.
- Incident investigations.
- Legal and regulatory updates.
- Changes to the business model.

Any participant may raise a potential risk through an appropriate reporting channel.

Material risks must be recorded even when the exact likelihood or impact is not yet known.

## 6. Risk register

The parent company must maintain a central risk register.

Each material risk should include:

- Unique risk reference.
- Risk title.
- Risk description.
- Risk category.
- Date identified.
- Identified source.
- Affected business areas.
- Potential consequences.
- Existing controls.
- Likelihood rating.
- Impact rating.
- Inherent risk rating.
- Planned treatment.
- Responsible risk owner.
- Target completion date.
- Residual risk rating.
- Current status.
- Last review date.

The register should distinguish risks from incidents.

A risk describes a possible future event. An incident records something that has already happened.

## 7. Risk assessment methodology

Each risk should be assessed using likelihood and impact.

### 7.1 Likelihood

| Rating | Description |
|---|---|
| 1 | Rare |
| 2 | Unlikely |
| 3 | Possible |
| 4 | Likely |
| 5 | Almost certain |

### 7.2 Impact

| Rating | Description |
|---|---|
| 1 | Negligible |
| 2 | Minor |
| 3 | Moderate |
| 4 | Major |
| 5 | Severe |

Impact should consider:

- Financial loss.
- Customer harm.
- Store Owner impact.
- Operational disruption.
- Legal exposure.
- Privacy consequences.
- Security consequences.
- Reputational damage.

### 7.3 Risk score

The initial risk score should be calculated as:

Risk score = Likelihood × Impact

| Score | Classification |
|---|---|
| 1–4 | Low |
| 5–9 | Moderate |
| 10–16 | High |
| 17–25 | Critical |

The numerical score supports prioritisation but does not replace professional judgement.

A low-frequency event with severe consequences may still require immediate controls.

## 8. Inherent and residual risk

Inherent risk is the exposure before considering existing controls.

Residual risk is the exposure remaining after existing controls and planned treatment are considered.

The register must not assume that a control is effective simply because it has been documented.

Control effectiveness should be assessed using evidence, testing and operational results.

Residual risk must be reassessed after material control changes.

## 9. Risk ownership

Every material risk must have an accountable owner.

Risk owners are responsible for:

- Understanding the risk.
- Maintaining an accurate assessment.
- Confirming existing controls.
- Proposing treatment actions.
- Monitoring changes.
- Reporting overdue actions.
- Escalating material deterioration.

The parent company retains responsibility for risks affecting its platform infrastructure and central operations.

Store Owners remain responsible for risks arising from their own business activities, products and fulfilment obligations, subject to applicable law and agreements.

Shared risks must have clearly defined responsibilities.

## 10. Risk treatment

Risk treatment may involve:

### Avoid

Stop or redesign an activity where the exposure cannot reasonably be accepted.

### Reduce

Introduce controls that reduce the likelihood or impact of the risk.

### Transfer or share

Use insurance, contractual arrangements or specialist service providers where appropriate.

Transfer does not eliminate the parent company's legal or governance responsibilities.

### Accept

Retain the risk where the exposure is understood and within approved risk tolerance.

Acceptance of significant risks must be documented and approved by an authorised decision-maker.

## 11. Risk appetite and tolerance

The parent company should establish and periodically review its risk appetite.

The following areas should generally have very low tolerance:

- Deliberate fraud.
- Bribery and corruption.
- Unauthorised access to sensitive information.
- Deliberate misuse of customer data.
- Unauthorised financial deductions.
- Serious product safety violations.
- Deliberate concealment of material incidents.

Other business risks may be accepted within defined limits where the potential benefits justify the exposure.

Risk acceptance must not override legal obligations or statutory protections.

## 12. Escalation

Risks must be escalated when:

- Their rating increases materially.
- Existing controls fail.
- Treatment actions become overdue.
- The potential impact becomes more serious.
- The risk exceeds approved tolerance.
- Multiple stores or critical systems are affected.
- A legal or regulatory obligation may be breached.

Critical risks must be escalated immediately to the appropriate senior decision-maker.

Escalation must identify the risk, potential impact, current controls and recommended action.

## 13. Risk monitoring and review

Risk owners should review their assigned risks at a frequency appropriate to their severity.

The parent company should conduct:

- Regular operational risk reviews.
- Monthly review of high and critical risks.
- Quarterly review of the overall risk register.
- Annual review of the risk management framework.

Reviews should also occur after significant incidents, acquisitions, major platform changes or changes to the commercial model.

## 14. Risk indicators

Where practical, the parent company should monitor indicators that provide early warning of increasing risk.

Examples include:

- Rising payment discrepancies.
- Increasing refund rates.
- Growing unresolved complaint volumes.
- Repeated platform outages.
- Increasing infrastructure costs.
- High Store Owner churn.
- Supplier delivery failures.
- Increased failed login attempts.
- Growing numbers of overdue security updates.
- Repeated inventory discrepancies.

Thresholds should be documented and reviewed rather than selected arbitrarily.

## 15. Relationship with incident management

When a risk materialises, the relevant incident management process must be activated.

The responsible team should:

1. Contain the immediate impact.
2. Protect affected customers and Store Owners.
3. Preserve relevant evidence.
4. Restore affected operations.
5. Investigate the underlying cause.
6. Identify control failures.
7. Update the risk register.
8. Implement corrective actions.
9. Monitor whether the risk has been reduced.

Incident closure does not automatically mean the underlying risk has been eliminated.

## 16. Third-party and supplier risks

Third-party risks must be assessed before entering material business relationships and monitored throughout the relationship.

Assessment should consider:

- Financial stability.
- Service reliability.
- Security practices.
- Privacy obligations.
- Regulatory compliance.
- Data access.
- Subcontracting arrangements.
- Business continuity.
- Exit and migration options.

Critical dependencies should have contingency arrangements where reasonably possible.

The parent company must understand the operational consequences of losing access to an essential service provider.

## 17. Store Owner risk awareness

Store Owners should receive relevant information about risks that could affect their stores.

This may include:

- Platform service interruptions.
- Payment-provider disruptions.
- Security concerns.
- Supplier issues.
- Product safety alerts.
- Material policy changes.
- Operational dependencies.

Store Owners should also be encouraged to identify risks within their own businesses.

The parent company should provide practical guidance without implying that it assumes responsibility for every independent Store Owner business risk.

## 18. Risk reporting

Management reporting should summarise:

- Critical and high risks.
- New risks.
- Changes in risk ratings.
- Overdue treatment actions.
- Failed controls.
- Significant incidents.
- Emerging threats.
- Risks requiring executive decisions.

Reports should identify the accountable owner and the action required.

Risk reporting must be accurate and must not hide unresolved exposure.

## 19. Implementation requirements

The parent company should establish:

- A central risk register.
- Defined risk categories.
- Consistent scoring methodology.
- Risk ownership assignments.
- Escalation procedures.
- Treatment tracking.
- Periodic review schedules.
- Management reporting.
- Links between risks and incidents.
- Evidence of control effectiveness.

Risk records should be protected according to the platform's information governance requirements.

## 20. Testing and assurance

The effectiveness of the framework should be evaluated through:

- Sample risk register reviews.
- Verification of risk ownership.
- Review of risk scoring consistency.
- Inspection of overdue actions.
- Testing of critical controls.
- Review of incident follow-up.
- Assessment of third-party dependencies.
- Comparison of reported risks against actual incidents.

Material gaps must result in documented corrective actions.

## 21. Review and maintenance

This policy should be reviewed annually and whenever material changes affect:

- The business model.
- Platform architecture.
- Regulatory obligations.
- Financial operations.
- Store Owner relationships.
- Supplier dependencies.
- Security threats.
- Organisational responsibilities.

Material changes must be approved by the appropriate authority and communicated to affected participants.

## 22. Governing principle

The parent company must understand the risks it takes, assign responsibility for managing them and act before avoidable problems become serious harm.

Risk management must support responsible growth while protecting customers, Store Owners, business partners and the long-term stability of the platform.
